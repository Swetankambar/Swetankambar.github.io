## How it started?
Well, to be honest I always wanted to run. And my first attempt at running was in 2016, I could run 5km in 30 minutes and that was it.
It was a brute force attempt to be "fit". It did not go so well because of a plathora of reasons, from not having any guidance, being physically weak to overexerting myself.
And then I got an injury and it stopped at 5km.

And in 2024 out of boredom I thought to run again. This time it was more structured. I joined a training program with a hope that I will find a hobby, and this has been one of the best leap of faith for myself.

## My learnings
This is my understanding after 2500kms of running. Since I started I made a lot of assumptions about every aspect of running and tried experimenting to verify my hypothesis. It is a continued effort and this blog sort of makes a checkpoint for me to record my current understanding about the sport, for my later self to look back.

## Running vs Strength Training

Ask a runner about this and it's likely they will say, yes, it is important. And then you ask them the weights they use at the gym. 
Then you ask the same to someone who has been doing hypertrophy OR crossfit kind of training for a while to see the reality.

For professional athletes the strength training generally lies in low weight high reps to build endurance. However, the same is not entirely true for an amateur runner (audiance for this blog). My observation has been that strength training is a definite way to gain pace and run injury free.

And then even the runners who agree on above tend to focus only on lower body workouts. 

That's leaving half of your body in starvation from the benefits of becoming stronger. And with age this becomes a bottleneck.

But then when one focuses on strength training there is fatigue which slows down the progress of running in a short term and that brings demotivation to the runner.

My solution, focus on the two aspects in cycle. Having even month long cycles when running takes a backseat w.r.t effort and focusing on very heavy lifting significantly improves the running form and the pace in the months to come, where one should focus on running centric exercises, gaining unilateral strength, mobility and strong lower body muscles. The cycle should be repeated every 3-4 months or so after a full fleged race effort.

## How frequently to race?

This one depends on the aim. If one is doing for getting more medals, run as frequently as one wants to.
If one does it for seeing new places, avoid the cities and choose trails and do as often as you want.

However, if one wants to improve pace in a road running scenario, the best way to go about it is to realise that the gains can't come too fast too soon.

After putting max effort in a race one needs to reset the system: nervous. muscular and cardiovascular, all of them. 
And then train for 4-6 months before attempting another PB for the same mileage. 

My rule of thumb: 2 HMs in a year, 2-3 10km races (Yet to run a FM but my idea would be 1 FM in a year with max effort).

## On Shoes

This is the subject we runners never get tired of discussing.
When I started, it was a barefoot shoe (no, not the fancy Vibram ones, it was a simple local product). And yes, I did have flat feet like a slab.
Poeple said not to do, but my legs were just at home with those shoes. At that time my timing was generally about 65 min for 10km in the practise runs.

In 3 months I went for a race and got about 53min for 10km. I was happy and my barefoot shoes just worked great. The strength training was helping my feet and calves to fully work and give me that pace.

Then I started training for HM with the same barefoot shoes, got a debut PB of sub 2 hour HM. This was 6 months since I started to run. This also felt pretty great and I thought never to switch to branded running shoes, thinking that its a rip off.

Then I got a desire to run fast. With that the practise became intense. And during interval runs I could feel the ground hitting my feet and sometime the roads hurt them.
With that I tried my first pair of shoes, Nike Pegasus 41. Oh, what a shoe! It was heavier than my barefoot shoes but gave a boost to me. 

One learning in this switch was that the engagement of my leg muscles was entirely different between the two shoes. It takes a while to get used to switch sides.
An outcome of this experiment was a learing about shoe rotation. 

No one shoe is ideal. One needs something with low stack height, low cushion, low support, light weight, fast with some plates and one also needs somethng with all things opposite. And the running plan should be such that all of them have a place in the journey.

My current rotation: 
* Puma DN3 (these are fast, have great grip, and are stable) - for my main events
* Sauconny ES4 (Similar to DN3 but have nylon plate and less stable and grip) - Used to be for events, now meant for intervals
* Asics Novablast 5 (The impact of rubber on feet is so much gentler than plater) - Long runs with varying tempo. My do it all.
* Saucony Kinvara 15 - My replacement for barefoot shoe. Very low stack height, heel drop, minimal support. It's so light that I keep shuffling in these. Supports great paces but I don't like it for more than 10km.

## How to remain injury free

First thing for this is to understand that too fast too soon is the most common cause of injury. So irrespective of the goal, ambition, one needs to be on lookout for any pain in the body. Maintaining a pain diary really helps. And the moment there is a niggle, go back to gym and anatomy basics and first sharpen the knife.
